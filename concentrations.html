Applied Physics Degree Paths

A concentration can be selected but is not required. If you declare one, it appears on your official transcript. Focus areas are unofficial planning tracks and are not listed on the transcript.

The program is taught by scientists from the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, NASA, and the Naval Research Laboratory. Coursework spans optics, interstellar space, ocean science, solid-state physics, and quantum mechanics, and is designed so you can tailor the degree to the work you do.

General M.S. Degree

The general Applied Physics master's path provides flexibility across core physics and engineering topics. Students complete four Applied Physics core courses (at least four from the first six listed options) plus six electives, with opportunities for independent study or project work.

This path is ideal for students who want a broad foundation without declaring a formal concentration on the transcript.

Materials and Condensed Matter

Combine concepts from the Applied Physics, Electrical and Computer Engineering, and Materials Science and Engineering programs for a well-rounded, holistic approach to applied physics. This path is for students working on how materials behave — from electronic and superconducting devices to sensors, nanomaterials, and materials in extreme environments.

Students build a foundation in mathematical methods, electromagnetics, statistical mechanics and thermodynamics, and materials science. Electives then move into solid-state physics, semiconductor devices, sensors and sensor systems, infrared sensors, superconducting devices, nanomaterials, mechanical properties of materials, electronic materials, polymeric materials, fatigue and fracture, and stress waves.

Typical structure: four Applied Physics core courses, then six electives with at least three (catalog: four or more) 700-level materials and condensed-matter courses from Applied Physics (615), ECE (525), or Materials Science (515/535). Remaining electives may come from Applied Physics or another EP program with advisor approval.

Applied Optics and Photonics

Listed as Photonics on the EP program page and in the academic catalog. Study applied physics, electrical and computer engineering, and photonics concepts for a defined approach to the practical application of light — lasers, optical systems, detectors, fiber-optic communications, imaging, and photonics hardware.

This concentration is the program’s optics path. Students move between theory and devices used in sensing, communications, and imaging. Common Applied Physics choices include Principles of Optics, Modern Optics, Optical System Design and Modelling, Optical Detectors and Applications, Optics and MATLAB, and Quantum Information Processing. ECE options include Fourier Techniques in Optics, Laser Fundamentals, Fundamentals of Photonics, fiber-optic systems, and related labs.

Typical structure: four Applied Physics core courses including EN.615.641 Mathematical Methods; one Electrical and Computer Engineering photonics core course; at least three 700-level Applied Physics optics/photonics electives; and two additional electives from Applied Physics or another EP program.

Space and Earth Science

This focus area applies physics to space systems, Earth and ocean science, heliophysics, planetary science, and astrophysics. Faculty ties to APL, NASA, and related missions make it a natural track for students working on remote sensing, climate and ocean physics, planetary exploration, and space systems.

It is built around two required cores — EN.615.744 Physics of Space Systems I and EN.675.613 Bold Science Enabled by Engineering — plus two additional Applied Physics core courses.

Typical structure: four core courses including the two required above; two Earth-science electives; two heliophysics and planetary electives; and two astrophysics electives. Up to two hardware courses — Sensors and Sensor Systems or Infrared Sensors — may substitute for other electives.

Quantum Engineering and Technology (forthcoming)

A quantum focus area is in development and is not yet published as an official focus area on the Courses page. Until it appears in the catalog, treat it as unofficial advising guidance only. The intent is to connect the mathematical foundation of quantum mechanics to quantum devices, algorithms, sensing, and information-processing systems.

Courses already in inventory on this path include Quantum Mechanics (615.654), Quantum Information Processing (615.781), and Quantum Algorithms: Near-Term Applications (615.776). Courses listed as in development on the Degree Pathways guide include Quantum Measurement and Sensing (615.685), Experimental Quantum Computing (615.783), and Introduction to Open Quantum Systems (615.786).

Plan 615.641 and 615.654 early and confirm the plan with the program chair or vice chair before treating this as a formal focus area. No individual map is available yet.
